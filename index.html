How small is a part per trillion?

The scale in one sentence
1 ppt = 1 second in 31,689 years

That is a ratio, not a health conclusion. To turn a concentration in water into a dose, you still need an exposure route, an intake rate, and body weight.

Why this matters

Environmental stories often report a concentration alone. The number can sound huge or tiny without context. This exhibit separates two questions: How dilute is it? and How much reaches a person?

Scale check: NIST metric/SI prefixes. Time conversions use 365.2425 days per year.

2. Put the ratios into drinking water

For dilute water, the density is close enough to 1 kg/L that these common concentration units line up approximately:

1 ppm≈ 1 mg/L
1 ppb≈ 1 µg/L
1 ppt≈ 1 ng/L
1 ppq≈ 1 pg/L
Water-unit check: U.S. EPA, Consumer Confidence Report units. These equivalences are not universal for every medium.

3. Turn concentration into a dose

Exposure route: drinking-water ingestion. Change the concentration, water intake, or body weight. The calculator uses the ATSDR structure D = (C × IR × EF) / BW, with EF = 1 for daily exposure.

Main takeaway: concentration and dose are different quantities. At the default assumptions, 1 ppt in drinking water = 2 ng swallowed per day = 2.86 × 10⁻⁸ mg/kg-day. The number changes when exposure assumptions change. A concentration alone therefore does not tell you the dose.

Sources, assumptions, reproduction, and AI use

How to reproduce the time visual: ppm = 10⁻⁶, ppb = 10⁻⁹, ppt = 10⁻¹², ppq = 10⁻¹⁵. Invert each ratio to get 10⁶, 10⁹, 10¹², and 10¹⁵ seconds, then convert seconds to days or years.

How to reproduce the dose example: convert the selected drinking-water concentration to mg/L; multiply by liters consumed per day; divide by body weight in kilograms. The calculator fixes exposure factor EF = 1 for daily exposure.

Important boundary: the ppm↔mg/L, ppb↔µg/L, ppt↔ng/L, and ppq↔pg/L equivalences here are for dilute water. “Parts per” can be defined differently in other media or contexts.
