### HIRE THE BEAVERS

What if we paid wildlife to create flood protections? 

### The idea:

My idea is to stop considering every beaver dam an evil, and some of them a form of public infrastructure. I want a "Beaver Payroll." A water authority would pay landowners to maintain appropriate beaver habitat and monitor wetlands on their land. Downstream communities, water utilities, and insurers could pay into the program whenever the wetlands are demonstrated to be having a quantifiable, positive impact. Beavers would be the contractors; people would be the accountants and safety experts.

It's not surprising that beavers create wetlands. It's not even surprising that they do it better. It's surprising that someone is asking if a wild animal can race an engineered structure to tap a public works fund. If a city is willing to pay a contractor to construct a stormwater basin, then why not pay a landowner to support a beaver meadow upstream if the data confirms that it works?

Why there is a problem worth solving:

Water travel through a landscape links flooding, summer dry stream beds and moist or degraded wetlands. An incised or otherwise straightened stream may move water downstream fast, but its floodplain connected counterpart can spread water out and even provide some to infiltrate into the ground. According to a fact sheet published by the U.S. Geological Survey in 2026, beaver dam analogs may temporarily delay runoff, promote infiltration and increase summer streamflow in areas experiencing declining snowpack [1].

There is also a good scientific argument to pay beavers some attention. A peer reviewed 2025 study modeled how restoring beavers might work in the Sierra Nevada [2]. Modeling suggested that we could get as much as 120 million m3 of surface water storage and 2,200 km2 of potential fire resilience effects under the model's assumptions. Those are modeling regional possibilities, not demonstrated outcomes, and they are not linked to any one dam preventing a flood. The Fish and Wildlife Service also describes benefits for floodplain connectivity and habitats and water tables [3].

I am not arguing that beavers will run around replacing concrete dams or stopping floods. The question is whether certain modest, well chosen projects could be so valuable locally that it would be worth someone paying for them.

How the program would actually work:

Step 1 In a handful of watersheds where beavers are natively present, where habitat is adequate, and where restoration wouldn't endanger roads, houses, protected habitat, or adjacent properties. Each site would be cleared by an independent hydrologist and wildlife agency prior to initiation.

Step 2 Landowners compete to provide the service: Landowners would not receive equal payments. They would post the minimum they would accept annually in exchange for land protection and beaver toleration. The lowest bids that are predicted to deliver the most benefit would be offered. This is similar to how companies ofering contracts for the lowest price to provide a service.

Step 3 Take concrete steps. Approved sites could use low tech starter structures or beaver dam analogs as suitable. Tree guards, culvert fencing, and pond level devices would safeguard property. The Fish and Wildlife Service highlight real coexistence projects using these techniques [4]. All species would remain un released unless authorities grant both the necessary permits.

Step 4 Check results before the big dollars. Stream gauges, aerial photos, visits to the site, and comparisons with other streams would record water level, peak flows, damage and habitat. Landowners would be paid for creating existing, approved habitat, with the performance payment based on the degree of measured benefit. Contractors would not be paid just for counting dams.

Step 5 Let the winners pay. A watershed authority could leverage public money with voluntary payments from water utilities, downstream property owners and insurers. Landowners would be paid out of that fund. An emergency reserve would pay for proven damage, and unsafe sites would be closed.

### The math: how big is a 100 site program?

All of this is meant as an estimate, not something based on a real project or published estimate. I'm working in round terms to make it easier to see if a proposal is winning or losing. All actual decisions would be based on measured site data and local bids.

### 1. Water storage: a crude physical estimate

Let's take 100 appropriate locations. Suppose an additional ponding of 0.5 acres at an average depth of 0.5 meter at each location. One acre = 4,047 sqm.

Water per site = 0.5 acre x 4,047 sq m/acre x 0.5 m 1,012 cu m.

On 100 sites = 1,012 × 100 = roughly 101,200 cubic meters, or approximately 26.7 million gallons of standing water in the US.

That sounds like a big number, but it is water already lying on the ground not new flood storage capacity. A pond that already is holding the maximum it can will have little or no room for the next rain. It can help slow down and divert some of the water, or soak it in, but that is measured separately.

### 2. The calculation that makes me question the idea

Supposing a severe storm delivers 5cm of rain into the 1 sq km of catchment above one location. Neglecting the effects of infiltration and losses, this would total 0.05m x 1,000,000 sq m = 50,000 cubic meters of rainfall.

The example pond is only about 1,012 m3 about 2% of that entire rainfall if one was empty, even if you were to fill it all up, or 1,012 / 50,000. And that pond's worth of directly available space is even less if the pond is already "full". And not every liter of rain becomes stream flow runoff. My estimate is a scale check, not an estimate of flood.

This is the very best reason NOT to over hype the results. The case for beavers simply can't be that a pond stores much water. It has to be that a wetland complex alters both time and damage in a quantifiable way.

### 3. How much would it be?

Suppose for this hypothetical 100 site program the up front cost for site studies, negotiations, initial work, safety controls, and equipment were $25,000/site. That translates to up front cost of 100 sites × $25,000/site = $2.50 million.

So, allow landowner payments of $6,000 per site, monitoring $2,500, maintenance/damage reserve $1,500, and admin $1,000. Total annual operating cost= $11,000 per site, or $1.10m for 100 sites.

To compare the up front investment to the yearly benefit, amortize it over 10 years using a 5% annual discount rate. The generic capital recovery factor is 0.1295, giving us an up front cost of $2,500,000 × 0.1295 = $323,800 per year.

Total annualized program cost=1,100,000+323,800=$1.424M =14,238/site/year The $25,000 setup cost and line items are my guesses, not contractor quotes.

### 4. When does the economics work?

The dollar value of benefits is the great uncertainty in the tradeoff. To figure the decision, first use $40,000 of avoided expected annual flood losses before restoration per site. (That has to be substantiated by the local flood risk assessment; it's not the average per property loss nationwide.) Then add in another $2,000 of avoided water quality or habitat benefits per site annually. That second figure is also hypothetical, and benefits can't be double counted.

Weak outcome: Flood damage falls only 10%.

Poor result: Flooding affects just 10%. Losses saved = $40,000 x 10% = $4,000 per site. Add in $2,000 other benefits makes $6,000 per site (or $600,000 in 100 sites). Comparing with $1.424 million in annualised costs that the program has to pay, this program is losing about $824,000 per year.

Midway result: Damage due to flooding drops 35%. Avoided losses = $40,000 x 35% = $14,000 per site. Plus $2,000 of other benefits to get up to $16,000 per site, or $1.60 million in total for the 100 sites. Net social benefit is around $176,000 per annum.

High result: Flood damages cut in half. Foregone costs = $40,000 × 50% = $20,000 per site. Add in $2,000 of other benefits for a total of $22,000 per site, or $2.20 million for 100 sites. Net benefit of this alone is about $776,000 per year.

Break even reduction is simply: ($14,238 annual cost minus $2,000 other benefits) ÷ $40,000 initial flood risk, which equals about 30.6%. This increase becomes 35.6% if the $2,000 side benefit is actually zero. (These are cut offs, not actual levels of flood reduction.)

Why this is sensible economically if it works

The externality is a positive one. The landowner experiences the inconvenience of flooded trees, wet ground, and restriction of land use, while a downstream resident perhaps receives the benefit of reduced damage or cleaner water. But no incentive exists for the owner to supply a benefit to another. Even if the entire net benefit to society is positive, market supply of the wetland may be less.

You will have solved that incentive problem if the reverse auction has the government estimate a value for each parcel. Any landowner with low opportunity costs could offer a bid lower than the government's estimate. Competitive bidding provides the same sort of information about which parcels are attractive that regulators typically lack. Paying based on performance also gives the program an incentive to avoid enrolling places that do nothing.

This is also a distribution question. If the primary benefactors are lower income neighborhoods further downstream that lack insurance coverage, public support may be more appropriate than requiring those households to pay their own limited capital. This Program should offer affected neighborhoods a say in site selection, not demand that rural landowners shoulder the entire burden of safeguarding city property.

### Why haven't we done this everywhere yet?

To be fair, the public has been restoring beaver habitat and constructing beaver dam analogs for years. This isn't a new biological discovery. Real projects are documented by National Park Service and Fish and Wildlife Service [3, 5]. What is new with my idea is to buy proven ecosystem services competitively through an ongoing watershed market instead of funds for a one time restoration project.

Why the concept has not become a normal public works contract is understandable. A local government might want an inspector controlled facility. An insurance company is not going to get lower premiums without evidence of lower risks. Landowners might be reluctant to accept restrictions. And of course, a beaver does not read an engineering plan. It might build in the wrong spot, shape the pond differently, or just leave. That uncertainty complicates even a straightforward engineering style performance guarantee.

Chesterton's Fence: what could we break by trying to change the system?

If we want to remove a feature such as the cleared drain, we need to understand why the feature is there before we remove it. Roads, straightened drains, levees and culverts can all be there to protect your home, your farm, your road, your boat and your ability to ride the local pony. Creating a wetland somewhere in the wrong place can undo those protections.

My plan, then, would retain significant flood control measures, beginning only at the point a competent team can assure us that they aren't sabotaged by restoring the ecosystem. It also provides for funding flow control devices and compensation. The USDA in particular has records showing beavers do flood roads and fields, kill trees, and erode banks or levees [6] all tangible expenses, not just "costs."

Biggest criticisms, unintended consequences, and safeguards

Dump on the wrong property. A wetland that buffers peak flow may also back up flooding onto property upstream. That shifts a cost, rather than avoiding it. Demand landowner and downstream neighbor agreement, site level floodplain maps, funded damage repair, and binding water level limits.

The wrong incentives. If the program pays for the number or size of the dams, it might incentivize dangerous ponding. Pay for independently verifiable outcomes, but reserve the right to walk away from dams that turn dangerous. If you have to take credit for wet years, use comparison reaches.

Ecological surprises. Waterlogging will kill some trees; ponding affects water temperature, fish movement, sediment movement, greenhouse gas emissions. Some effects are local or adverse. Must have ecological assessment, wildlife permits, water rights review and monitoring for expansion.

Political fallout. Flood control contractors, drainage districts, property owners, insurers, and conservation organizations might disagree. In some areas they would be out of work or face more expensive insurance. In others, they would get additional business. Keep contracts open and do not dedicate them to a perfect substitute for traditional flood control.

How I would test this without blowing cash

I'd start with 10 sites, not 100. First, take a pre project baseline for each stream and pick alike, untreated comparison streams. Then monitor streamflow timing, water level, actual damage, habitat, maintenance costs, and complaints in neighboring streams for at least three years. A separate review would then try to tell whether any observed change yields a reduction in expected annual damages. If it can clear a safety test and an independent cost benefit analysis, the program grows.

One successful pilot still takes "several wet and dry years of implementation" in order to defend a claim of flood risk. FEMA and the Government Accountability Office explain why a premium based on expected annual loss is a long term average of damages, not a forecast of damages for next year [7]. Two years free of storms would not be proof of concept.

### Conclusion

Initially, the notion of paying for beaver dams seems like a joke. I believe the more relevant question is what are we buying with public money for the environment? We want less damage, more effective water management, more resilient ecosystems. If beavers can give us some of those things more cost effectively than a different project, then I do not see why they should be discounted because of their nature.

Yet the figures don't directly back up what I am suggesting. Even my example makes a loss if flood losses only drop by ten percent, and the tiny pond in the storm scenario is overwhelmed by a significant rainfall. The potential is the upside worth exploring just because we are uncertain. I would see this as a contest between options: compare actual results, only pay for actual benefits, defend the risk takers and be ready to pull the plug when the numbers don't add up.
