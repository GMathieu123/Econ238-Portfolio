What Does 10 Minutes of Idling Really Cost?

A running parked car creates two different costs: one the driver pays directly, and one that falls outside the gasoline purchase. This exhibit puts both on the same scale.

Environmental-economics point: gasoline expenditure is a private marginal cost. The climate damage associated with the extra CO₂ is an external cost. A marginal decision should compare the benefit of another minute of idling with both costs—not assume the activity is automatically good or bad.

How to reproduce it

Fuel = idling rate × minutes ÷ 60

Private cost = fuel × gasoline price

CO₂ = fuel × 8.887 kg CO₂/gallon

Climate external cost = (CO₂ kg ÷ 1,000) × SC-CO₂

Break-even benefit = private cost + modeled climate external cost

Kick the tires

Fuel-use uncertainty

DOE gives roughly 0.25–0.50 gallon/hour for parked idling. At the same gasoline price, that alone doubles the private cost and CO₂ result.

Valuing climate damages

EPA's 2026 SC-CO₂ values range from $133 to $365 per metric ton across the 2.5%, 2.0%, and 1.5% near-term Ramsey discount-rate cases. The environmental cost therefore depends on an economic assumption, not just chemistry.

What this does—and does not—show

This exhibit does not claim that every minute of idling should stop. Idling can provide heat, air conditioning, visibility, safety, or convenience. It also does not monetize local air pollution, noise, engine wear, starter wear, or other effects. The model intentionally isolates one private cost and one external cost so the tradeoff can be seen clearly.

Next question: If those additional benefits and costs were added, when would the marginal social benefit of another minute of idling exceed its marginal social cost?

Assumptions

The default idling rate is 0.375 gal/hour, the midpoint of DOE's 0.25–0.50 range. The $3.50/gallon gasoline price is illustrative and adjustable. The default $215/t SC-CO₂ is EPA's 2026 value at the 2.0% near-term Ramsey discount rate. The exhibit reports tailpipe CO₂ only and treats the EPA social-cost estimate as a modeled external cost, not a literal fee charged to a driver.

What AI was used to do

AI was used to locate and compare the DOE and EPA source material, check unit conversions, calculate low/central/high scenarios, test how the results change when assumptions change, and build the interactive public exhibit. The underlying source values, formulas, and assumptions are displayed so another reader can reproduce the calculation and change the model.
